> [!WARNING] Scope and conventions
>
> I am using circuits as the guiding picture throughout, but the story is really about structure. The same ideas apply to classical circuits, linear-optical circuits, dataflow graphs, and string-diagram calculi. And when I mention quantum circuits here, I mean unitary circuits only: no measurement, discarding, reset, or classical branching. Those features are important, but they live in richer categorical settings than the one I want today.

If I were explaining category theory to a friend, I would start the same way I would start explaining phase in quantum computing: not with the full formalism, but with the one practical question that makes the formalism unavoidable.

For phase, that question is something like: why does a number you cannot directly read off from a measurement still matter so much for interference?

For categories, the question is even simpler: what is the right language for things you can plug together?

A circuit fragment already has the shape of an answer. It has some wires coming in, some wires going out, and the most important thing you can do with it is connect it to another fragment whose interface matches. Once you notice that, category theory stops looking like a zoo of abstractions and starts looking like careful bookkeeping for a habit you already had.

## Start with circuits, not axioms

Suppose you have a box $f:A\to B$ and another box $g:B\to C$. You can plug the first into the second and get a composite $g\circ f:A\to C$. You also want a do-nothing box $\mathrm{id}_A:A\to A$ for each interface $A$, and you want the obvious sanity conditions: if you compose three boxes, the answer should not depend on where you place the brackets, and plugging in a do-nothing box should do nothing.

That is a category.

Formally, a category $\mathcal C$ has objects $A,B,C,\dots$, morphisms $f:A\to B$, a composition law, and identities, satisfying
$$
h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f,\qquad
f\circ \mathrm{id}_A=f,\qquad
\mathrm{id}_B\circ f=f.
$$
But I think the better way to hold the idea in your head is this: objects are interfaces, morphisms are processes, and composition means "do one after the other."

Once you say it that way, the standard examples feel much less exotic. **Set** is the category of sets and functions. **Vect$_k$** is the category of vector spaces over $k$ and linear maps. A poset can be viewed as a category where there is at most one arrow between any two objects, and a monoid can be viewed as a category with exactly one object. That last example is the first hint that algebraic structure can often be repackaged as compositional structure.

That repackaging is basically why category theory was invented. Eilenberg and Mac Lane were not trying to make mathematics sound grander. They needed a precise way to talk about constructions in algebraic topology that kept behaving "the same way" across different settings. Category theory began as a way of keeping structure and structure-preserving maps under control before it became anyone's philosophy of mathematics.

That is also why applied category theory has spread so widely. The same discipline (keep interfaces explicit, make composition primitive, separate local pieces from global structure) turns out to be useful in databases, networked systems, stock-and-flow models, and current work on formal methods for AI safety. Quantum circuits are just one especially vivid place where the diagrams make the compositional logic impossible to miss.

There is, though, an immediate limitation. A plain category only knows one way to combine things: first this, then that. Circuits also have another move that is just as basic: put two pieces side by side and let them run in parallel.

That extra move is exactly what a **monoidal category** gives you. It equips the category with a tensor product $\otimes$. On objects, $A\otimes B$ means "system $A$ together with system $B$." On morphisms, $f\otimes g$ means "do $f$ on one part and $g$ on the other." There is also a unit object $I$, which you can read as "no system at all."

This is where string diagrams suddenly make the theory feel inevitable. Wires represent objects. Boxes represent morphisms. Vertical stacking is ordinary composition. Horizontal juxtaposition is tensor. A lot of proofs become literally diagrammatic: slide this box, straighten that wire, swap those components. Penrose's tensor diagrams and the later coherence theorems of Joyal and Street are the reason this style of reasoning is not just a convenient picture but honest mathematics.

Now tighten the picture further. Suppose the only objects you allow are the natural numbers
$$
0,1,2,\dots
$$
and you interpret object $n$ as "$n$ wires." Suppose also that tensor on objects is just addition:
$$
m\otimes n := m+n.
$$
Then a morphism $f:m\to n$ is nothing more mysterious than a box with $m$ inputs and $n$ outputs. A strict monoidal category of exactly this kind is called a **PRO**.

This is already very close to circuit syntax. The objects are not carrying subtle semantic information; they are just counting arity. A gate with two inputs and one output is a morphism $2\to 1$. A state is a morphism $0\to 1$. An effect is a morphism $1\to 0$. And because everything lives inside a monoidal category, you can still compose sequentially and place things side by side.

One reason PROs matter is that they let ordinary algebraic theories become diagrammatic rewrite systems. A monoid, for example, has a multiplication $\mu:2\to 1$ and a unit $\eta:0\to 1$, subject to the equations
$$
\mu\circ(\mu\otimes \mathrm{id})=\mu\circ(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mu)
$$
and
$$
\mu\circ(\eta\otimes \mathrm{id})=\mathrm{id}
=\mu\circ(\mathrm{id}\otimes\eta).
$$
Inside a PRO, these are not abstract formulas floating in isolation. They are equations between wiring diagrams. In other words, "an algebraic theory" can be understood as "a presented diagrammatic language."

The only thing a PRO still does not give you automatically is the ability to swap wires. It is planar. Left and right really do matter unless you add more structure. But most circuit languages absolutely do let you permute wires. Categorically, that is symmetry. A **PROP** is a PRO with coherent permutation maps, or equivalently a strict symmetric monoidal category whose objects are the natural numbers.

That tiny addition makes the match with ordinary circuit reasoning much tighter. Object $n$ means $n$ wires, or $n$ qubits, or $n$ qudits. Tensor means "put these subcircuits next to each other." The symmetry maps are SWAPs and wire permutations. And the actual gate library is described by generators together with equations. So, at the level of syntax and rewriting, a circuit language is very often best understood as a **presented PROP**: choose generating gates, state relations between diagrams, and then reason modulo those relations.

This is also the right level to think about completeness. A complete equational theory is telling you that the rewrite rules you wrote down really are enough to capture every true identity in the fragment you care about. And one of the nicest categorical morals in this area is that completeness sometimes appears only after you translate to another compositional language where the structure is easier to see. Instead of attacking one circuit syntax in total isolation, you build the right bridge and let the translation carry the hard part.

So far, though, the story is still mostly about wiring plus gate equations. That is enough for a lot of circuit reasoning, but it starts to feel cramped as soon as control enters the picture.

## The place where ordinary PROP presentations start to creak

When we say "controlled gate" in casual quantum-circuit language, it is easy to imagine that control is just one more family in the gate zoo. Controlled-$X$ gives CNOT. Controlled-$Z$ gives CZ. Two controls and you get Toffoli. But that way of talking hides the real pattern.

Control is not most naturally a gate. It is an operation you perform on gates and on whole circuits.

Given a unitary $U$, you can build a controlled version of $U$. Given a composite circuit, you can build a controlled version of the composite. Given that controlled circuit, you can add another control. The pattern is open-ended. That is exactly why rewrite theories start to sprawl when control is treated as nothing more than a derived gadget: the moment you allow arbitrarily many controls, you begin to need arbitrarily many control-specific identities.

So here is the conceptual move that makes everything cleaner:

> treat control as structure, not as a pile of special cases.

The payoff is immediate. Instead of writing separate rules saying "this equation still holds when both sides are positively controlled" or "this identity still holds under one more control wire," you want a single structural principle:
$$
f=g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C(f)=C(g).
$$
That is a huge compression of a rewrite system.

One elegant way to formalize this, due to Delorme and Perdrix, starts with a PROP $P$ and looks at the subcategory $P_{\mathrm{endo}}$ of endomorphisms, meaning morphisms of the form $n\to n$. A **control functor** is a functor
$$
C:P_{\mathrm{endo}}\to P_{\mathrm{endo}}
$$
that sends each object $n$ to $1+n$. In plain language, it adds one control wire. On a morphism $f:n\to n$, it produces a controlled version
$$
C(f):1+n\to 1+n.
$$

The crucial word there is "functor." Because $C$ is a functor, two of the identities you desperately want are automatic:
$$
C(\mathrm{id})=\mathrm{id},\qquad
C(g\circ f)=C(g)\circ C(f).
$$
So "control respects doing nothing" and "control distributes over sequential composition" are not ad hoc rules for each gate family. They come for free from the structure. The full definition includes coherence laws saying that nested controls interact properly with one another and with wire permutations, but the heart of it is very simple: a controlled PROP is a PROP equipped with a disciplined way of adjoining a control wire.

In finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, the usual semantic example is the familiar block-diagonal construction. For qubits, controlling a unitary $U$ on the state $\lvert 1\rangle$ gives
$$
C_{\lvert 1\rangle}(U)
=
\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes I
+
\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert\otimes U.
$$
For qudits of dimension $d$, you can control on any basis value $\lvert k\rangle$:
$$
C_{\lvert k\rangle}(U)
=
\lvert k\rangle\langle k\rvert\otimes U
+
\sum_{\ell\neq k}\lvert \ell\rangle\langle \ell\rvert\otimes I.
$$
This is the usual "apply $U$ only on the chosen control branch" picture. What the categorical language adds is not a new matrix formula; it adds a way of recognizing that all such formulas instantiate a common structural idea.

A particularly nice feature of the Delorme-Perdrix setup is the notion of **points** for a control functor: special states $0\to 1$ that behave like abstract "false" and "true." Plug one of them into the control wire and the controlled circuit collapses either to the identity or to the underlying operation. That captures the logic of control without tying it to any one preferred matrix representation.

And once you think of control that way, it becomes natural to notice that there need not be only one control convention. Even for qubits, you might want to control on $\lvert 1\rangle$, or on $\lvert 0\rangle$, or on another distinguished state related by conjugation. For qudits, the basis states give you a whole family of canonical controls. So instead of one control functor $C$, you may want a family
$$
(C_k)_{k\in[d]}.
$$
That is the basic intuition behind **polycontrolled PROPs**: control comes in a family, and the interesting questions are no longer just "how do I define it?" but "how do the different controls interact, commute, and cover the available cases?"

There is a complementary viewpoint, due to Heunen, Kaarsgaard, and Lemonnier, that reaches a similar conclusion from a different angle. They begin with what they call a **controllable prop**, or crop: a prop of endomorphisms equipped with a distinguished involution $x:1\to 1$, which you can think of as NOT, satisfying $x^2=\mathrm{id}$. From there they freely adjoin controlled versions of morphisms.

What is striking in that approach is how small the control theory becomes. They work with two endofunctors, positive control $C_1$ and negative control $C_0$, and show that a short list of eight equations is enough to govern the behaviour of control. Those equations express exactly the things you would hope for: functoriality, compatibility with extra idle wires, the relation between positive and negative control via NOT, complementarity, commutativity, and the relevant swap coherences.

I think that result is philosophically important because it makes the main point impossible to miss. Control is not really a million unrelated gadgets called controlled-something. It is a very small structural theory that can sit on top of a base circuit theory.

And that brings us back to rewriting. A PROP already helps because it separates wiring facts from gate-specific facts. A controlled PROP goes one step further: it separates ordinary gate equations from control equations. Instead of teaching the rewrite system, over and over again, that a valid identity remains valid under added controls, you make that lifting behaviour part of the language itself.

In areas where complete axiomatisations of quantum circuits naturally want infinitely many controlled equations, that shift is more than an aesthetic cleanup. It changes what a local rewrite system can even look like. The dream is no longer to memorize every multi-controlled special case. The dream is to state a compact structural theory from which those cases fall out automatically.

That is why I find the categorical story compelling. Category theory did not win a place in circuit reasoning by imposing some alien taste for abstraction. It won because circuits were already compositional. They already had interfaces. They already had sequential and parallel composition. They already had wire permutations. They already had equations between diagrams. The categorical language just names those facts clearly enough that we can use them.

Seen from that angle, the progression is very natural. Categories capture "plug this into that." Monoidal categories add "do these side by side." PROs turn arity into objects. PROPs add the symmetries that real wiring needs. And controlled PROPs acknowledge that control is not a mere afterthought in the gate set but a structural operation on circuits themselves.

Once you say that out loud, a lot of rewrite theory starts to look less like a bag of clever tricks and more like the ordinary consequence of choosing the right language.

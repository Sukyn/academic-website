Here’s a thing I didn’t realize for an embarrassingly long time:

> We never really learned how to **multiply vectors**.  
> We learned several *projections* of a multiplication.

- The **dot product** $a\cdot b$ spits out a **scalar** (a number).
- The **cross product** $a\times b$ spits out a **vector**… but only in 3D, and it’s secretly a pseudovector.
- Matrix–vector multiplication is really “apply a linear map,” not “multiply two vectors.”

**Geometric algebra** (a.k.a. *Clifford algebra with a geometric interpretation*) gives a clean answer:

> Multiply vectors by keeping *both* the symmetric and antisymmetric information.

And that “both at once” viewpoint ends up being extremely natural for quantum computing, because Pauli operators, rotations, Clifford structure, and a lot of diagrammatic reasoning already live in the same neighborhood.

> [!WARNING] Scope and conventions
>
> I’ll focus on **real** geometric algebra over Euclidean space (signature $(n,0)$) for intuition.
> When you see $Cl(p,q)$, that’s the same construction but with a metric that has $p$ “$+1$ squares” and $q$ “$-1$ squares”.
>
> Also: “Clifford algebra” (an algebra) is not the same object as the “Clifford group” (a group of gates), even though they are tightly related in quantum computing.

## 1. What is a vector?

A vector is (at least) three compatible things:

1. an **arrow** (direction + magnitude),
2. a **linear functional machine** (it acts by dotting against other vectors),
3. an element of a vector space that transforms linearly.

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, we usually fix an orthonormal basis $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ and write
$$
a = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i e_i.
$$

So far, nothing about multiplication: vectors add and scale.

---

## 2. Why “vector multiplication” is awkward in standard vector calculus

### 2.1 Dot product: “how aligned?”
The dot product is
$$
a\cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta,
$$
a scalar that measures alignment.

### 2.2 Cross product: “which way is the perpendicular?”
In $\mathbb{R}^3$ you can define
$$
a\times b,
$$
a vector orthogonal to both.

But this is already a warning sign:
- it only exists *canonically* in 3D (and 7D for more exotic reasons),
- it depends on a choice of orientation,
- it is secretly encoding an **oriented area element** (a plane segment), not an honest vector of the same kind as $a$ and $b$.

So the cross product is “kind of the right second half” of a multiplication… but it’s forced to masquerade as a vector.

---

## 3. Bivectors: the missing object

The “thing” you actually want to represent “the oriented plane spanned by $a$ and $b$” is a **bivector**.

### 3.1 The wedge product
Geometric algebra starts by taking seriously the **outer product**
$$
a\wedge b,
$$
which is:
- **antisymmetric**: $a\wedge b = -\,b\wedge a$,
- **zero if parallel**: $a\wedge a = 0$,
- geometrically: an **oriented parallelogram** (a 2D subspace element).

The magnitude of $a\wedge b$ is the area:
$$
\|a\wedge b\| = \|a\|\,\|b\|\sin\theta.
$$

So:
- dot product measures the **cos** part,
- wedge product measures the **sin** part.

That’s already suspiciously close to “polar form.”

### 3.2 Grades: scalars, vectors, bivectors, trivectors…
Geometric algebra is *graded*. You get a direct sum of “$k$-vector” pieces:
- grade 0: **scalars**
- grade 1: **vectors**
- grade 2: **bivectors**
- grade 3: **trivectors** (oriented volumes)
- …
- grade $n$: the **pseudoscalar** (an oriented $n$-volume element)

A general element is a **multivector**: a sum of mixed grades.

## 4. The geometric product: multiplying vectors for real

Here’s the punchline.

### 4.1 Definition by what it should do
We want a product $ab$ that:
- is associative,
- distributes over addition,
- encodes the metric via $a^2 = \|a\|^2$ (in Euclidean signature).

From these, one can characterize the key identity:
$$
ab + ba = 2(a\cdot b).
$$

That immediately suggests splitting the product into symmetric and antisymmetric parts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a\cdot b &:= \frac12(ab+ba) \quad\text{(scalar)}\\
a\wedge b &:= \frac12(ab-ba) \quad\text{(bivector)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the **geometric product** decomposes as:
$$
\boxed{ab = a\cdot b + a\wedge b.}
$$

That’s it. That’s “how to multiply vectors.”

### 4.2 A 2D computation you can do by hand
In 2D, take orthonormal basis $e_1,e_2$ with
$$
e_1^2=e_2^2=1,\qquad e_1e_2=-e_2e_1.
$$

Let $a=a_1e_1+a_2e_2$ and $b=b_1e_1+b_2e_2$. Expand:

$$
ab = (a_1b_1+a_2b_2)\;+\;(a_1b_2-a_2b_1)\;e_1e_2.
$$

- The scalar part $(a_1b_1+a_2b_2)$ is **exactly** $a\cdot b$.
- The bivector coefficient $(a_1b_2-a_2b_1)$ is the **signed area** (a determinant).

And the bivector $I:=e_1e_2$ satisfies
$$
I^2 = (e_1e_2)(e_1e_2) = -1.
$$

So in 2D, the geometric algebra looks like **complex numbers**:
$$
a \;\leftrightarrow\; a_1 + a_2 I,
$$
and geometric multiplication *is* complex multiplication.

> [!INFO] Intuition checkpoint
>
> In 2D, the “imaginary unit” is not magic.
> It’s literally the oriented unit area element $e_1e_2$.

### 4.3 In 3D: cross product reappears as a dual
In 3D, the bivector space is still 3-dimensional (it has basis $e_1e_2, e_2e_3, e_3e_1$), and the pseudoscalar
$$
I := e_1e_2e_3
$$
squares to $-1$ and (in 3D) commutes with everything.

Using duality, the cross product is essentially “bivector turned sideways”:
$$
a\times b \;\propto\; (a\wedge b)\,I^{-1},
$$
(up to a conventional sign depending on orientation conventions).

So geometric algebra doesn’t throw the cross product away; it explains what it *really* is.

## 5. Rotations are products: reflections and rotors

This is where the multiplication starts paying rent.

### 5.1 Reflection is sandwiching
Let $n$ be a **unit** vector normal to a plane ($n^2=1$). Reflect $v$ across that plane by:
$$
v' = - nvn.
$$

This is an honest formula inside the algebra — no matrices required.

### 5.2 Rotation is two reflections
A rotation is the composition of two reflections.
If $m,n$ are unit vectors, then
$$
R := mn
$$
is a **rotor** (an even multivector), and the rotated vector is
$$
v' = Rv\widetilde{R},
$$
where $\widetilde{R}$ is the **reverse** (reverse the order of vectors in products).

### 5.3 Exponentials of bivectors
If $B$ is a unit bivector with $B^2=-1$, then the rotor for a rotation by angle $\theta$ in the plane of $B$ is:
$$
R = e^{-B\theta/2} = \cos(\theta/2) - B\sin(\theta/2).
$$

This is the geometric algebra version of “$e^{i\theta}$ rotates the plane.”

## 6. Why this feels *very* quantum

Quantum computing is already saturated with:
- Pauli operators,
- rotations on the Bloch sphere,
- Clifford structure (normalizing Paulis),
- diagrammatic calculi (ZX, ZH, etc.).

Geometric algebra is basically the same geometry, but with a different “primitive language.”

### 6.1 Pauli operators as a Clifford algebra in disguise
The Pauli matrices satisfy the same “Clifford” relation:
$$
\sigma_i\sigma_j + \sigma_j\sigma_i = 2\delta_{ij}I.
$$

So the algebra generated by $\{\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z\}$ is a matrix representation of a geometric algebra (often identified with $Cl(3)$).

### 6.2 One-qubit unitaries are rotors
A standard physics formula is:
$$
U(\theta,\hat n) = e^{-i\,\theta\,(\hat n\cdot \sigma)/2}.
$$

Geometric-algebra translation:
- the complex $i$ behaves like a pseudoscalar,
- $\hat n$ is a unit vector direction,
- the exponent is a **bivector** generating a rotation.

So “single-qubit rotation” is literally “rotate a vector by sandwiching with a rotor.”

This makes the Bloch sphere picture feel less like a metaphor and more like “the actual algebra.”

> [!TIP] Mental model
>
> - **Vectors** represent axes / observables (think Pauli directions).
> - **Bivectors** represent planes of rotation (think rotation generators).
> - **Rotors** represent the rotations themselves (think SU(2) elements).
>
> That’s basically “Bloch sphere mechanics,” but in an algebra where multiplication is geometric.

## 7. The graphical-language angle: generalized Clifford algebras and braids

Now to the paper I wanted to connect this to:

**Robert Lin, “A Graphical Calculus for Quantum Computing with Multiple Qudits using Generalized Clifford Algebras”**.

Very roughly (and focusing on the “why I care” angle), the paper:
- builds a **graphical calculus** for multi-qudit computation from a **generalized Clifford algebra**,
- uses algebraic identities to prove diagrammatic moves (including a **Yang–Baxter** relation),
- produces a **braid group representation** from these algebraic braid elements,
- and argues these braid operators are **2-local**, so (at least in principle) they look implementable as quantum gates.

A phrase in the abstract that jumped out at me:

> the braid elements are *almost Clifford gates* — they normalize the generalized Pauli group up to an extra root-of-unity factor.

This is exactly the kind of statement that screams “graphical rewriting + algebraic structure are doing the same job.”

### 7.1 Why this resonates with circuit reasoning
If you’ve been thinking in terms of:
- **PROPs / presentations** (“generators + relations”),
- **rewriting systems** (“local moves that preserve semantics”),
- and “control as structure,”

then this paper is another example of the same pattern:

> pick an algebraic backbone (here: generalized Clifford algebra),  
> choose a diagrammatic syntax,  
> prove the key relations (here: braid/Yang–Baxter style moves),  
> and you get a **graphical reasoning engine** for a family of computations.

Even if you don’t plan to use *this* calculus directly, it’s a great demonstration of the strategy:
**algebra ⇒ diagrams ⇒ rewriting ⇒ computation.**

## 8. Cheat sheet

| Concept | In words | In formulas |
|---|---|---|
| scalar | magnitude-like | grade 0 |
| vector | direction-like | grade 1 |
| bivector | oriented plane element | $a\wedge b$ |
| geometric product | “multiply vectors” | $ab=a\cdot b + a\wedge b$ |
| inner product | symmetric part | $a\cdot b = \tfrac12(ab+ba)$ |
| outer product | antisymmetric part | $a\wedge b=\tfrac12(ab-ba)$ |
| rotor | rotation element | $R=e^{-B\theta/2}$ |
| rotate vector | sandwich action | $v' = Rv\widetilde{R}$ |
